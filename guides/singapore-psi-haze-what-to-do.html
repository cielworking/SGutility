Singapore PSI and Haze Today: What the Numbers Mean and What to Do

How to read NEA’s 24-hour PSI and 1-hour PM2.5 bands, what MOH advises for healthy and vulnerable people, and where to check live regional readings.

Updated 6 November 2026

When the sky looks milky or your chat groups start posting PSI screenshots, the useful question is not “is there haze?” — it is which number to use, and what MOH/NEA advise for your next few hours versus tomorrow. This guide explains Singapore’s official air-quality readings in plain English and points you to live regional numbers on SG Alerts haze.

Two numbers, two jobs

NEA publishes both a 24-hour PSI and a 1-hour PM2.5 concentration. They answer different planning questions.

The 24-hour PSI is an index built from six pollutants (including PM2.5 and PM10). NEA’s FAQs explain that a sub-index is computed for each pollutant and the highest sub-index becomes the PSI. A PSI of 101 and above is described as unhealthy. Source: NEA air pollution FAQs; haze.gov.sg.

The 1-hour PM2.5 reading (µg/m³) reflects more recent conditions. MOH’s haze page says to use it when deciding immediate outdoor activity over the next hour, and to use the PSI forecast when planning for the next day. Source: MOH — Haze.

Third-party “AQI” apps often use different scales. For Singapore health advice, stick to NEA PSI / PM2.5 and MOH’s tables — not a foreign AQI colour you cannot map cleanly.

PSI bands (for next-day planning)

NEA and MOH publish these PSI descriptors:

  • 0–50: Good
  • 51–100: Moderate
  • 101–200: Unhealthy
  • 201–300: Very Unhealthy
  • Above 300: Hazardous

MOH’s advisory table (also mirrored in NEA FAQs) summarises outdoor-activity guidance by group:

  • Healthy persons: normal activity in Good/Moderate; reduce prolonged or strenuous outdoor exertion when Unhealthy; avoid prolonged/strenuous exertion when Very Unhealthy; minimise outdoor activity when Hazardous.
  • Elderly, pregnant women, children: normal in Good/Moderate; minimise prolonged/strenuous exertion when Unhealthy; minimise outdoor activity when Very Unhealthy; avoid outdoor activity when Hazardous.
  • Persons with chronic lung or heart disease: normal in Good/Moderate; avoid prolonged/strenuous exertion when Unhealthy; avoid outdoor activity when Very Unhealthy or Hazardous.

MOH defines the verbs in plain language: prolonged = continuous exposure for several hours; strenuous = involving a lot of energy; reduce / minimise / avoid as stated on the page. Source: MOH haze advisory.

1-hour PM2.5 bands (for the next hour)

MOH and NEA’s haze materials group 1-hour PM2.5 into four bands. Approximate thresholds published on those pages:

  • Band 1 Normal (0–55 µg/m³): continue normal activities.
  • Band 2 Elevated (56–150): healthy persons — reduce strenuous outdoor activity for the next hour; vulnerable persons — avoid strenuous outdoor activity for the next hour.
  • Band 3 High (151–250): healthy persons — avoid strenuous outdoor activity for the next hour; vulnerable persons — avoid all outdoor activity for the next hour; may wear a mask to reduce exposure.
  • Band 4 Very High (≥251): healthy persons — minimise all outdoor activity for the next hour; may wear a mask; vulnerable persons — avoid all outdoor activity for the next hour; may wear a mask.

Vulnerable persons in these guides include the elderly, pregnant women, children, and people with chronic lung or heart disease. MOH notes the personal guide is not meant to be rigidly prescriptive, and that mild irritation for healthy people often settles after leaving the exposure. Sources: MOH haze page; NEA haze poster PDF linked from haze.gov.sg.

Masks, indoors, and when to see a doctor

MOH states that for healthy individuals, short-term haze exposure generally does not cause major health issues beyond eye, nose or throat irritation. N95 masks (or equivalent) are not needed for ordinary commuting from home to school or work, and are not needed indoors. When 1-hour PM2.5 is in Band 4, healthy people who must be outdoors for several hours may reduce exposure with a well-fitted N95 (or equivalent). Vulnerable persons who must go outdoors in Band 3 or 4 may also consider a mask as described on the MOH page.

MOH also advises drinking water to stay hydrated, and that people who are unwell should seek medical attention. This guide does not diagnose symptoms — follow MOH wording and your doctor.

Regional readings vs a single island number

NEA reports PSI and PM2.5 by region (North, South, East, West, Central). A “Singapore” headline figure may hide a hotter West or clearer East. On SG Alerts haze, pick the region that matches where you will actually be outdoors for the next hour — school, worksite, or stadium — instead of reacting only to the worst screenshot in a chat group.

NEA’s FAQs also explain how sustained 1-hour PM2.5 bands relate to 24-hour PSI ranges if those bands persisted all day (for example, Elevated band conditions aligning with an Unhealthy PSI range). That relationship is a teaching aid, not a forecast engine: always read the live values rather than reverse-engineering yesterday’s chart.

What “good enough” looks like for common plans

  • Commute / short outdoor errands: follow MOH’s note that healthy people usually need not wear N95 masks for ordinary home-to-work or home-to-school trips; still check Band 3–4 if you are vulnerable.
  • PE, runs, cycling: use the 1-hour PM2.5 band first; step down intensity when MOH says reduce/avoid strenuous activity.
  • Next-day events: use PSI forecast bands the evening before, then re-check 1-hour PM2.5 on the morning itself.

A simple check routine on a hazy day

  1. Open SG Alerts — Singapore haze (or haze.gov.sg / myENV) and note both regional 24-hour PSI and 1-hour PM2.5, plus the source time.
  2. For plans in the next hour (PE, outdoor run, children’s play), use the 1-hour PM2.5 band.
  3. For tomorrow’s picnic or sports day, use the PSI forecast / advisory bands.
  4. Adjust by health status — vulnerable groups should follow the stricter column in MOH’s tables.
  5. Pair with rain radar & forecast if wet weather is also in play; rain and haze are separate tools.

Bottom line: PSI tells you how the last day’s air quality indexes; 1-hour PM2.5 tells you what to do in the next hour. Read MOH’s bands, then check the live regional numbers — do not invent “safe hours” that NEA never published.
