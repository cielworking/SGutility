How to Read HDB BTO Application Rates

What HDB’s BTO application rate means (applications ÷ public supply), how to read household-type columns and the median, and what the rate does not tell you.

Updated 6 November 2026

During every BTO sales window, HDB publishes application rates that update several times a day. Chat groups treat a single decimal like a lottery ticket. This guide explains what that number actually is — using HDB’s own formula and footnotes — so you can read the table without inventing “% chance of getting a flat”.

It complements (and does not replace) our February 2027 BTO / HFE deadline guide. You still need a valid HFE letter before you can apply.

HDB’s definition (the only one that matters)

On HDB’s “Flat Supply & Applications Received” pages, application rates for each household type are defined as:

Application rate = number of applications received for that household type ÷ public flat supply set aside for that household type (rate rounded to one decimal place; supply rounded to a whole number).

Source example for the November 2026 exercise notes: HDB — Flat Supply & Applications Received. The same definition appears on other exercise pages.

So a first-timer-family rate of 2.6 on a 4-room category means about 2.6 applications for each public flat set aside for that household type in that project/flat type — not a guarantee that “everyone in 2.6 gets shortlisted,” and not a national average.

Read your column, not someone else’s

HDB breaks rates out by household type (for example first-timer families vs second-timer families on 3-room and bigger flats; seniors and first-timer singles appear in 2-Room Flexi tables). Quota shares differ for Standard vs Plus/Prime flats — HDB lists the minimum percentages set aside for each group on the same notes page.

Always match:

  • the project and flat type you intend to apply for,
  • your household type column,
  • and whether the project is Standard / Plus / Prime as labelled on the Flat Portal.

Comparing a second-timer rate to a first-timer rate, or a 2-room Flexi seniors rate to a 4-room first-timer rate, is mixing different queues.

What the rate is not

  • Not your booking probability. HDB shortlists more applicants than the flat supply. Its notes state that, taking quotas into account, shortlisted applicants may be more than 100% and potentially up to 200% of supply; shortlisted applicants get a queue number and may book if those ahead drop out.
  • Not a pure “new” demand signal. HDB notes that applications include people who applied in previous launches and may still be waiting to book, which on average form about 20% of total applications.
  • Not final until the window closes. During the application period, HDB updates counts at stated clock times (for the November 2026 exercise: 8am, 12pm and 5pm daily, with a final update after close). Always read the “as at” timestamp on the page.

Source: Important Notes on HDB’s application-rate page.

Median rate and HDB’s own tip

HDB publishes a median application rate: the 50th percentile across flat application categories (half the categories sit above it, half below). Its notes encourage applicants who want to improve chances of success to look for categories with rates below the median. That is HDB’s published tip — not a promise that every below-median application succeeds.

CNA’s coverage of the November 2026 launch similarly relayed HDB’s encouragement to monitor rates and consider projects with lower rates (around one or lower was mentioned in that reporting). Prefer the live HDB table over screenshots. Source for launch context: CNA — November 2026 BTO.

How to use the table during a live exercise

  1. Confirm you have a valid HFE letter (see HFE deadline guide for February 2027 planning).
  2. Open HDB’s Flat Supply & Applications Received page for the current exercise and note the as-at time.
  3. Find your project, flat type, and household-type column.
  4. Compare that rate to the published median for the exercise.
  5. Cross-check project details on the HDB Flat Portal and track launch status on SG Alerts BTO launches.
  6. Remember selection is by computer ballot, not first-come-first-served (HDB’s standard sales rule).

Worked example (method only — numbers change every update)

Suppose HDB’s table shows, for a given Standard project’s 4-room row, first-timer families at rate R with a published exercise median of M. Your reading steps are:

  1. Confirm you are a first-timer family applying for that exact 4-room category.
  2. Note the page’s “Number of Applications Received as at …” timestamp.
  3. If R is below M, HDB’s published tip says that category is on the less crowded half of the board — still not a booking guarantee.
  4. If R is far above M, expect a more contested shortlist for that column; consider whether another flat type or project with a lower rate fits your needs.

We deliberately do not paste live Nov 2026 decimals into this evergreen explainer; they go stale within hours. Open HDB’s live application-rate URL for the current exercise whenever you decide.

Where SG Alerts fits

BTO launches helps you see which exercises and towns are on the radar. It does not replace HDB’s official rate table during a live window. Use both: SG Alerts for orientation, HDB for the authoritative applications ÷ supply figures.

Common misreads to avoid

  • Treating a town-wide “buzz” as your flat-type rate.
  • Assuming rate 1.0 means every applicant gets a key collection date — shortlisting and drop-outs still sit between application and booking.
  • Ignoring that previous-launch applicants are inside the count (~20% on average per HDB’s note).
  • Refreshing once at noon and never again — rates move through the window.

Bottom line: An application rate is applications ÷ public supply for your household type. Use it to compare relative demand, follow HDB’s median tip if you want a less crowded category, and never convert the decimal into a fake success percentage.
